# Design Foundations

Use these lenses as questions, not universal formulas.

## Player experience

Start with what the player should notice, feel, understand, decide, or become
capable of doing. Separate that intended experience from theme, story premise,
feature list, and implementation technology.

Ask:

- What is the player repeatedly doing?
- What makes one choice meaningfully different from another?
- What information is available when the choice is made?
- What consequence teaches the player what happened?
- What tension, curiosity, mastery, expression, cooperation, or surprise
  sustains attention?

## Mechanics and dynamics

A mechanic is a rule or action available in the system. Dynamics are patterns
that emerge when mechanics interact over time. The experienced result may
differ from the designer's intention.

Express a mechanic hypothesis as:

```text
If the player can <action> under <constraint>,
then they may <behavior or strategy>,
which may create <intended experience>,
unless <credible countereffect>.
```

## Meaningful choice

A choice tends to become more meaningful when the player understands enough to
form an intention, alternatives involve real trade-offs, consequences are
legible, and no option dominates in every relevant situation. Hidden
information can still support meaningful choice when uncertainty itself is
intentional and learnable.

## Feedback and learning

Players form models of a game through feedback. Review whether feedback is
timely, perceptible, interpretable, and connected to the player's action. Do
not confuse punishment with clarity or reward volume with motivation.

## Challenge and failure

Ask what ability the challenge tests, whether failure teaches anything, how
recovery works, and whether difficulty comes from meaningful demands or merely
friction. Accessibility changes the path to participation without requiring
the design to abandon its intended challenge.

## Progression and rewards

Distinguish skill growth, knowledge growth, expressive range, content access,
power growth, status, and accumulation. A reward system can redirect attention
away from the experience it was meant to support. Review compulsion,
manipulation, gambling-like uncertainty, and pay-related pressure explicitly.

## Systems and balance

Balance is contextual. Look for dominant strategies, non-decisions, runaway
advantages, stalled states, brittle dependencies, and roles with unequal
agency. Prefer testable balance questions over a claim that all options must be
numerically equal.

## Theme and narrative

Theme can shape how mechanics are interpreted. Narrative can be authored,
systemic, emergent, environmental, or player-created. Check whether the rules
reinforce, complicate, or contradict what the game says it is about.

## Evidence discipline

Use existing games as examples, not proof that the same design will work in a
different context. Treat designer intention, player report, observed behavior,
retention data, commercial performance, and cultural interpretation as
different evidence classes.
